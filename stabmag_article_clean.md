## Excerpt

Multiple bankruptcies, Russian oligarchs, environmental fugitives and a… wavepool?


---

Editor’s note: Readers, strap in for this one. A dozen interviews, hundreds of legal documents and 5,000 words later, Billy Wilson has delivered one of Stab’s most in-depth investigations to date. The fact that it focuses on such an obscure, peripheral sliver of the surfing world (sorry Britain) — and hardly even sniffs a sea breeze — makes it all the more impressive. If you’ve got some spare mushies lying around the house, now is the time to take them. If, at any point while reading, you find yourself confused and/or dazed, just do as the Britons do and keep calm an carry on. Lastly, apologies in advance if your name is Nick. 

When Jaydee, a South African surfer now living in Ireland, arrived at The Wave, an inland surf lake on the outskirts of Bristol, the metal entrance gates were locked. Propped against them was a whiteboard, with that day’s date – 26th June, a Thursday – scrawled in marker pen along the top, and underneath it this message:

“Due to circumstances beyond our control The Wave is closed to all visitors today. We are incredibly sorry for any inconvenience caused and are working hard to rectify the issues and reopen as soon as possible.”

He’d received no warning, only a generic “pre-arrival” email the day before regarding the on-site accommodation, where he and his family had a booking for the night (£120). Their sessions in the pool were booked for the following day. Jaydee noticed the website was down on Thursday morning when he tried to check the water temperature, but the only other hint that trouble might be afoot came on the flight over, when he caught a glimpse of a suspiciously flat-looking wavepool out the window.

With The Wave closed, he made some last-minute rearrangements and headed with his family that evening to London. When he finally got into bed, Jaydee checked his phone again: “There it was. The email. The one giving us the heads up that things had gone pear shit. Sent just before midnight.”

“Pear shit” is a good one.

Who travels to surf a wavepool, and why? Jaydee, a handy surfer to judge by his Instagram, was bought his sessions at The Wave by his wife as a 50th birthday present, he explained via DM. Even beforehand he hadn’t been overly stoked on the idea, thinking of all the logistical costs on top, and joked to his wife that he “probably could’ve flown to the Maldives for a week for a bit extra”.

By strange coincidence, Stab friend and contributor Ryan Miller was also due to be at The Wave that Thursday. He was on holiday with his daughter, and had just been in Liverpool to see Dua Lipa play – twice, two nights in a row. When he got word that the Bristol pool was shut, his first instinct was to go there anyway – it would be up and running again soon, and they might even get to “score” it empty. But his man on the ground told him not to bother. He couldn’t say what had gone wrong, but it definitely wasn’t mechanical.

Miller loves wavepools, by the way, and is only half-joking when he says his goal is to surf every wavepool in the world. “I love taking turns,” he said on the phone to us. “Love not battling for waves, I just love everything about them.” When he was in UAE for the Surf Abu Dhabi Pro, Miller visited a neighbouring emirate to surf a different wavepool –  “Dion’s pool” from Globe’s 2012 short film “Electric Blue Heaven”, the one with all the Russian models frolicking in the shallows. Miller got the impression his booking might have been their first in a while. The models had cleared out. He caught one wave and then the machine broke.

Ryan takes this sort of thing in stride, and escaped relatively lightly from the Bristol fiasco. Others were less fortunate. Vince Graham is usually based in the Bay Area but was in France this June, and decided to combine an inland strike mish with a very different sort of musical experience. Slayer was playing Cardiff, so he paid for a week’s accommodation in Bristol, an hour away on the M4. (Vince actually plays in a band called Borderwars, the side project of Slayer’s drummer. Their latest single: the appropriately titled “I’ll Piss on Your Grave”.)

Customers were told – when The Wave emailed them back a month later – that their cancelled sessions could be rescheduled for free, but there would be no refunds. Which is fine if you live in Weston-Super-Mare, less so if you live in the Netherlands – like Martine, who travelled from Amsterdam by train, in keeping with The Wave’s environmentalist spiel (this ought to have earned her some sort of discount in itself). She and her friends abandoned their three-day trip after the first day.

I even heard from someone who’d come all the way from Mexico, as part of a Spanish-language group that runs coaching trips around the world. He chose the three-day Bristol option partly because the dates meant he could drop in on the Cannes Lions advertising festival en route. But the main reason was to get the reps in, and because the waves were guaranteed. Except that on the morning of the third day, he learned that this was not in fact the case.

The real mystery, though, isn’t the people who surf wavepools but the people who build them, who own them.

Pear shit

“Due to circumstances beyond our control,” read the whiteboard at the gates. Even at the time, when photos of that sign were first shared online, I remember thinking it a rather comical phrase. The whole point of a wavepool, after all, is having control of all the circumstances.

The message ended by advising people to “keep an eye on our socials, website and your emails for more information”. Those who followed this advice witnessed in real time a wonderfully chaotic exercise in reputation management and executive squabbling. Allegations of various kinds of illegality (including the hacking of accounts and data) were posted on The Wave’s social media, then almost immediately deleted. It was panic stations, as the English are fond of saying.

“A ransom attack is suspected but at this time the attacking party is yet to make their demands” was arguably the highlight. “The police have been notified.” (An interesting 999 call, one imagines. Buckley, on a recent Stab podcast, described that last phrase as “the best thing to say when you’re lying”.) A few minutes later the post was gone. The Wave appeared to be suffering from a kind of corporate schizophrenia, until finally the new management was able to change the logins and make the mean voices go away.

Stab covered this at the time under the headline “Britain’s First Wavepool Has Closed – What Really Happened”, even if it didn’t quite make good on those three words after the dash. It was “a messy financial standoff” between two feuding parties, correctly identified as a private equity firm called Sullivan Street Partners (which until recently was the majority owner), and a group called Jar Wave Ltd (which until recently wasn’t, and still sort of isn’t).

The first part of that headline was also slightly misleading, by the way, albeit technically true. Britain’s first wavepool has closed, it just isn’t this one. Surf Snowdonia, set amid the breath-taking valleys and glacial landforms of Wales’ largest national park, has been flat now for two years, rendered obsolete by sexier technology. A Hilton was built alongside it in 2021, but the King Suite with Lagoon View wasn’t the hit they’d anticipated, and for a while the whole hotel was being used by the government to house asylum seekers (which sounds like the premise of a terrific North Shore sequel). Then it closed down and was sold off by administrators as part of a “pre-packaged sale”. (If you don’t know what that is, don’t worry. You’re going to love them.)

By the time it was published on 1 July, the Stab article had also been rendered obsolete: The Wave was open again and had new ownership, in the form of a company called Sea Level Wave Co. The circumstances were back under control, even if the website was broken and phone calls and emails weren’t being answered. With the booking system down, for several weeks there was no alternative but to turn up and pay on the door, just like in the good old days – a jarring mix of old technology and new. (The booking system is now working again.)

What really really happened in Bristol, though? And who was controlling the circumstances? I made the mistake of trying to find out. Scroll through the public filings and you’re soon drawn into a warren of financial connections between endlessly proliferating subsidiaries. Swiss bankers, London money men and Russian wrestlers scurry at your approach. Finally you emerge, not necessarily any the wiser, into a strange kind of shadow world where everyone seems to be called Nick.

The first Nick is the deepest

Nick Hounsfield, an osteopath by trade, set out nearly 15 years ago on a mission to heal the nation via the medium of inland surfing. Driven by his belief in “blue health” and the “triple bottom line” of people, planet and profit, he secured funding for The Wave, which opened for business in late 2019 – the first pool in the world to use Wavegarden’s Cove technology. It reportedly cost around £30m to build, and took almost as many litres of drinking water (kinder on the machinery) to fill it.

I never entirely bought the idea that the way to restore humanity’s frayed relationship with nature was to concrete over it and build in its place a resource-intensive imitation. To be fair, when I interviewed Hounsfield on the phone shortly before The Wave first opened, he managed to make it sound almost plausible. He was a true believer, adamant this “ocean-like environment” would be “the perfect classroom to engage with a mass audience about these issues”.

The CEO for the first few years was a chap who’s not called Nick, and who’s now taking care of the triple bottom line in Bahrain, working on an unrelated wavepool project. Hounsfield was the vibes man, the Chief Visionary Officer. Occasionally he laid the visionary stuff on a bit thick. I remember an aerial photo of The Wave posted to his Instagram with the caption: “Every great dream begins with a dreamer. Always remember, you have within you the strength, the patience, and the passion to reach for the stars to change the world.” Stirring words, attributed to the “incredible abolitionist” Harriet Tubman. The parallel between the freeing of slaves and the emancipation of Britain’s landlocked surfers had never occurred to me until then.

Obviously, I still wanted a go on it. At the time I was working in marketing for an online retailer – a medium-size blot on my copybook – that became a partner of The Wave’s. At some point we were invited to come and have a look, and I stood on the rooftop viewing platform, heard the rumble of machinery, and saw the day’s first set summoned from the perfectly still waters, shiny and reflective in the morning light. Yes, it was impressive – vision-like, indeed – and Hounsfield, standing nearby, was justifiably proud of the waves he had set in motion. He saw that they were fair to good.

A jar of worms

Who bankrolled the vision? In 2018, the aforementioned Jar Wave began lending money to The Wave Group, which passed the money on to its subsidiary, The Wave (or Surf Bristol Ltd, as the operating company was officially called). These details aren’t very interesting in themselves, but may help explain the confusing fact that the Bristol pool, though called The Wave, has been wrested away from the The Wave Group, which is now Wave-less.

Sullivan Street, who didn’t get involved until 2022 and remain The Wave Group’s owner, have described Jar Wave as “a private investment vehicle set up for the wealthy Asheshov family”. But seemingly in the driving seat of the vehicle, at least to begin with, was a British-based banker called Francis Menassa. He already had a long and tangled string of such vehicles to his name, all of them beginning with “Jar”.

The initial Jar Wave loans totalled £15m and were “secured” against the pool itself; in the event of a default, in other words, Jar Wave would have certain rights to the facility and certain ways of enforcing them. (This, in a roundabout sort of way, is what has now happened.) For some reason – possibly just to confuse a financially illiterate surf journalist several years down the track – about a third of the loans were funnelled, if that’s the word, through a separate Jar entity based in Gibraltar.

You still with us?

Menassa was more of a management type, and earlier in his career worked for Credit Suisse, one of the two main banks that helped make Switzerland the world-leader in complex financial structures. By the time he got involved in the wavepool game he was among the UK’s most highly regarded private-client advisers, and enjoyed disseminating financial wisdom in blog posts with titles like “Revolutionary New Surfing Technology is Creating the Perfect Wave for Investors”.

Even when he was pontificating on US monetary policy, surf-adjacent imagery tended to muscle its way into the picture: “How Macroeconomic Tides Are Changing” read another Menassa headline, above a stock photo of an unridden and in this case ocean-generated wave. (It seems entirely possible that surfing’s ready store of bland, investor-friendly metaphor is largely responsible for the wavepool boom.)

Then, in 2023, Menassa went bankrupt, and was asked to step out of the vehicle, sir. Bloomberg UK described the fall of the “once-feted London money manager” as the result of “bad bets” on wave pools and, yes, crypto. Pretend waves and pretend money. Your heart bleeds.

As a consequence Jar Wave had its assets frozen, which meant that for the next 19 months it was held in a state of suspended animation, unable to receive interest payments. When the “freezing order” was lifted late last year, the vehicle came under new control and swiftly began moving through the gears.

The real Francis Menassa: once-feted finance guy steps into surfing, promptly goes bankrupt. A reflection of the times?

2-3ft and mostly offshore

Some companies in the extended Jar family are registered in the UK, others in Switzerland or Gibraltar; many have been wound up by the liquidators, but a few are still plugging away, doing whatever it is they do. The etymology is disputed. Menassa liked to say he took the first letter of the first name of each of his three children and together they spelled out J-A-R. Heart-warming stuff.

And yet the website of a firm called Jar Capital – which was reportedly shut down in 2023, but seems to be going great guns in Switzerland still – lists the founder as an old colleague of Menassa’s, and suggests a different origin story: Jar “signifies the fusion of timeless wisdom and cutting-edge innovation”. No doubt you’re wondering how. “Just as ancient jars safeguarded life’s treasures,” goes the explanation, “we preserve and grow your wealth with the precision of modern finance.” It was around this point in my investigation that I began to think the old sort of jar was a better idea.

Public filings provide the odd clue as to whose money has at various points been in the jar labelled “Bristol”. In January 2021, for example, a security was created in connection to a £1.5m loan from someone called Konstantin Sintsov. He’s an interesting google if you have a spare 12 hours – a keen amateur wrestler and the co-founder of Rustranscom, one of Russia’s largest railway operators. In 2019 he and his partner sold a controlling stake to the state-owned Russian bank VTB for $400m. 

Big Kon. A man you can trust.

According to a list of shareholders from a few years ago, most of the actual shares in Jar Wave were held either anonymously in the British Virgin Islands or by one of several companies registered in London under the Asheshov name. Three Jar entities, meanwhile, held between them around a quarter. A large chunk of England’s first inland surf park was thus owned offshore, which I think has to count as an irony.

That surname Asheshov belongs to the second Nick, the “other Nick”. A mutual friend described him to me as a “wonderful man” with a genuine love for surfing. This second Nick was already toying with the idea of a London wave pool when he met the first Nick. The details of the Jar Wave loans were hashed out and work began on the Bristol pool, the plan being to follow it with one in London.

Asheshov used to be an equity sales director in the investment arm of VTB, the aforementioned Russian bank. When I spoke to him on the phone this summer, he explained that he returned to the UK (his home country) shortly after “it all started going wrong, in 2014”. When Putin annexed Crimea, “you could see it was only going in one direction – the society and the country”.

What of the “wealthy Asheshov family” mentioned by Sullivan Street? Nick takes his surname from his mother, Anna, who competed for Great Britain in the 1964 Winter Olympics (38th in the Downhill). His father, Michel Feron, was also a top skier, but represented Belgium. The family business was scrap metal. When Michel and his brother Denis, another Winter Olympian, expanded their European operations to the States, they founded a company called Chimico, later Chemetco, in Illinois. It became one of the world’s largest copper refiners.

This was how Denis ended up on the US Environmental Protection Agency’s list of environmental fugitives. According to the federal indictment he had a secret waste pipe installed, which discharged zinc-oxide slurry into a tributary of the Mississippi for years until its discovery in the mid-‘90s. (In 2010, an elderly Denis struck a deal with prosecutors, who dropped the charges in return for a $500K fine and acknowledgement of responsibility.) Cleanup of the site is ongoing, says the EPA.

Well, you can’t help who you’re related to, and possibly Denis was just a naughty uncle who never got invited round for Christmas again. Asheshov says he never met him – “there was a split in the family many years ago” – and confirmed via email that “there is absolutely no connection with Denis Feron’s Chemetco and any family wealth or funding that has gone into The Wave at any point”.

The old Chemetco site, famed for refining copper, infamous for its environmental ruin.

Diversity, private equity & inclusion

The dedicated Stab reader, always thinking of the bigger picture, might at this point interject that copper has to come from somewhere, and isn’t it better to recycle it than to dig it fresh out the ground on the coast of, say, Sumbawa? That reader might go further and say that money has to come from somewhere, too, and a private equity fund isn’t so much a jar as a black box – even more anonymous, even less accountable.

Sullivan Street describe themselves on their website as “a conviction investor seeking complex situations in the lower-mid market”. If that popped up on your dating app, you might think you were dealing with a bit of a creep. At any rate you probably wouldn’t swipe right.

But what if they had an “initial funding platform of c.£27 million”? What if you were a complex situation? Late 2019 was, in hindsight, a disastrous time to open a leisure facility. The UK went into lockdown just a few months later.

Thus Sullivan Street became the majority shareholder of The Wave Group in 2022, “with Jar Capital remaining as a significant investment partner”, as the press release put it. What could go wrong? Hounsfield, who remained as a director of The Wave Group, affirmed the new investors’ “commitment to sustainability and creating social change”. Layton Tamberlin, one of the two founding partners, celebrated the “wellness and health benefits to people across the country”. No pre-nup was signed.

For a while the bromance flourished, and in 2023 around six acres of solar panels – a similar footprint to that of the pool itself – were installed in a neighbouring field. Generating more energy than the pool consumes, these panels made the business a net contributor to the power grid. Good news for the triple bottom line.

See? Solar panels. That makes everything better. Crisis averted.

Yet some customers detected a certain vibe-souring. Efficiencies were made, prices increased (up to £79 an hour for the more advanced settings), and the clientele changed to match the prices. On an unofficial Facebook group, one poster seemed to sum up the general mood when he wrote that the place had been “taken over by corporate greed and run into the ground”.

Earlier this year, revised plans were unveiled for a £50m-plus facility in North London, as was a contract with Endless Surf to provide the tech. Perhaps Sullivan Street took their eye off the Bristol ball. In any event, on 26th June the pool closed, Jar Wave having “opted to appear to attempt to take control of the business”, to quote The Wave Group’s syntactically challenged press release from that day.

The sale was completed the day after, leaving Sullivan Street in the slightly awkward position of trying to raise funds for a hypothetical wavepool while being dispossessed of an existing one. A series of emails sent to The Wave Group’s mailing list in July explained that they didn’t have “any of the monies” from pre-purchased items and were no longer liable for them, but that a crowdfunding campaign would be launching soon in case anyone wanted to give them some more monies. If Sullivan Street were after a complex situation, you can’t fault their Wave selection.

“The guys that are pumping money into these pools aren’t stupid,” Ryan Miller told me. The venerable Nick Carroll, on the other hand, has called it a “hilarious business model… Never has so much been spent chasing so little” (as quoted by Ben Mondy, another Stab friend and contributor, and surfing’s foremost wavepool beat reporter).

Like Larkin and Amis, or Johnson and Gove, Sullivan Street’s two founding partners met as students at Oxford. These are smart people. Some years ago Layton Tamberlin had a letter about French literature published in the culture pages of The Guardian (“Ferdinand Mount should surely have mentioned Simone de Beauvoir’s Les Mandarins…”), and the syntax was first-rate. Curiously, he specified not just the city but the correctional facility he was writing from, namely Wandsworth Prison. The Telegraph article about how he wound up there – finance expert jailed after fleeing crash in flaming car while impressively over the limit – meanwhile vanished from the internet. All of which is merely to confirm that smart people are capable of great stupidity.

Ryan Miller. Loves wavepools. Hated by airlines.

The circumstances

The exact sequence of events that culminated in this summer’s acrimonious falling-out is convoluted and contested, and to try and untangle it here would be futile. What follows is an abridged account. If phrases like “long-term value creation” send a rush of blood to your non-thinking parts, you are in for a treat.

“Several parties have pursued personal returns, significantly acting out of step with moral and fiduciary duties,” read one of the deleted Instagram posts, rather cryptically, on Friday 27th June. Administrators had been appointed, the pool sold, assets “appropriated illegally”. The post ended on a note of defiance, even optimism: “We march on and are determined to continue our mission to deliver an exciting inland surf experience for all.”

Meanwhile Sullivan Street made a statement to Greatest Hits Radio (naturally) that called out Asheshov by name. It alleged that while Jar Wave was under the freezing order, he agreed that “cash earmarked for interest payments could instead be invested into long-term value creation”. When the freezing order was lifted late last year, he “abruptly reversed his position, claiming he never had authority to agree to any of the above”. (Sullivan Street described Asheshov as “a long-time board director”, but public filings show he only became a director post-freezing order.) With Sullivan Street unwilling and/or unable to pay the money demanded of them, Jar Wave went about enforcing the security on the pool.

So much controversy for such quaint little British cottages.

The statement was also notable for introducing another Nick to proceedings. Asheshov’s actions were “coordinated with his cousin Nicholas Feron, now based in Switzerland”, and had “exposed their true intention: for management to deploy company cash, and then to use legal manoeuvres to take control of the business at a discount.” (That “now based in Switzerland” is a nice touch.)

The thing about “legal manoeuvres” is that they are generally speaking… um… legal. The administrators who approved them have written several lengthy reports, which describe how the pool was bought by Sea Level Wave Co (SLWC) as part of a “pre-packaged sale”. This is a common if somewhat controversial mechanism, whereby the assets of an insolvent business are sold via an “accelerated marketing process”. The winning bid, worth just over £14m, was made up of a few hundred thousand pounds cash and £13.66m in credit. In other words, the bidder agreed to assume the debt to Jar Wave, which thus retained its security. Unsecured creditors, i.e. other businesses owed money by The Wave at the time of the sale, seem unlikely to get anything much back.

The administrators make no mention of any prior agreement regarding earmarked cash. (A corporate lawyer friend said that if they spent it without a legal contract in place, Sullivan Street were “legit being negligent”, and would themselves have been acting out of step with a “fiduciary duty to protect their clients’ investments”.) They do mention several rival bids from Sullivan Street, but only one of these offered a full repayment to Jar Wave. It fell through because the paperwork wasn’t returned by the deadline, leaving the administrators with no option, in their words, but to transact the SLWC offer. (Someone at The Wave Group who didn’t want to be named said they were “definitely not told” about any deadline.)

SLWC was set up in early April – a day before Jar Wave issued its formal demand for payment – by Julian Topham, who used to run the events company that expanded Boardmasters, Newquay’s long-running QS contest, into the full-blown music festival it is today. He’s also the chairman of Surfing England, the national governing body, having taken over from Nick Hounsfield late last year.

Curious, I got in touch with Hounsfield, who resigned from all Wave-related companies in April. His resignation, he told me over email, means he’s “not allowed to work in the surf park sector for a long time”. He and Topham have known each other for years, he said, but it wasn’t until June that he learned of Topham’s bid. As for why he resigned, he said this: “I realised that as a director of The Wave Group we were making decisions which were in conflict with what was best for The Wave Bristol. That, to me, was incredibly distressing and was literally making me ill.” (Several years ago, shortly after The Wave opened, Hounsfield suffered a series of strokes.)

We march on

Who, though, is Nicholas Feron, the third Nick (fourth if we include Carroll, who’s probably mixed up in this somehow)? As far as I can tell he is a sentient being in his own right and not some kind of human subsidiary. Public filings show he’s the director of a holding company called Fernhope Ltd, and used to be on the board of AuVert Mining Group, a specialist in “eco-friendly mining solutions”. The latter was founded in 2017 by James (Jimmy?) Slade, a presumably reformed character who’d just got out of prison for violating the Clean Water Act in the US. (Fernhope was an early investor, but the partnership ended prematurely following what I can only describe as a messy financial standoff.)

Fernhope’s main trading subsidiary, Multimetco, is a metal refining company in Alabama; its only other director is Patrice Feron, the majority owner, but Nicholas signs the accounts these days. According to these accounts, Multimetco typically enjoys a turnover in the hundreds of millions, but the macroeconomic tides are changing. After 40 years in business, it ceased its core operations on 30th June this year.

Public filings also show that on 30th June, Feron became a minority shareholder in SLWC, but The Wave’s PR man assured me this was merely “part of an ongoing corporate transaction”. Neither of us really knew what that meant, but apparently the transaction’s now over and Feron no longer has a stake in the company (as is reflected in the filings). Asheshov told me that Feron, though now a director, owns no shares in Jar Wave, and stressed that Julian Topham “is the sole shareholder and owner” of SLWC.

What now for The Wave? Bristol is the sole focus, Topham said via email, and the “overriding ethos is to ensure it is accessible and affordable for all” (prices for advanced sessions are down to £55). Bundles are being honoured, “even allowing for the fact that nearly all the payments were made to the previous operating company”. When I asked who customers like Jaydee and co ought to be angry with, he took a similar line: “They paid the previous operating company, which was responsible for The Wave going into administration, and who closed The Wave on 25th June.” (The Wave Group source said the closure was due to the automatic expiry of their contract, which Jar Wave and the administrators chose not to extend, and thus not their fault.)

Green pastures, unfortunately, don’t always lead to happy endings.

What the two sides do agree on is the field of solar panels. These belong to The Wave Group outright, but they “aren’t central to the running of The Wave so are not covered by the administration”, as Topham put it. As such they generated no energy over the British summer, which seems rather a shame, and as good a symbol as any to end on.

So perhaps we are back where we started, with our old friend the messy financial standoff. This is more or less Hounsfield’s take: “two parties have split up due to irreconcilable differences. It’s never easy, but everyone needs to move on and do the best they can for the future.”

The future, it now seems likely, will feature a wavepool in Lee Valley Park, several miles northeast of Tottenham Hotspur Stadium. Recently The Wave Group changed their name to Crest Experiences, and in late September were granted planning permission for what will now be called Surf London – “a new brand for a new future”. They hope to begin construction next year and to open in 2027. If you would like to own a slice of inland surf park, they are currently accepting investments.

As for all the claims and counter-claims, it would require a sharper legal mind than mine to offer an authoritative opinion. I am confident that all involved have acted honourably under the circumstances, which after all were beyond their control. Who or what was in control? Some combination of impersonal forces greater than ourselves, most likely. Providence, nature, the sea gods of old? Huey, mana? Some other mystical entity, based in Switzerland? 

No, none of the above. The great engine of human progress is my guess. The invisible hand of the market, in loose alliance with the machines. We march on.
